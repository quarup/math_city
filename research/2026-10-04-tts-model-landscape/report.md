# Pre-render the letters, keep the OS reading questions

**Bottom line.** In October 2026 Math City's best move is a split: keep `flutter_tts` and the OS engines for the seed-generated question prompts, but pre-render the finite set of citizen letters offline with a permissively licensed 2026 "voice design" model (Qwen3-TTS-VoiceDesign, Apache-2.0) so each of the ten citizens gets a distinct, designed, consistent voice at zero runtime cost and a few megabytes of bundled audio. The models that can ship inside the app are a short list of 15–100M-parameter networks (Kokoro-82M, Piper, KittenTTS, Kyutai Pocket TTS, the now-archived Supertonic), every one of which carries a licence or maintenance trap; the hosted frontier (Eleven v4, Gemini 3.8 Flash TTS, Cartesia Sonic 3.6) is roughly 260 Elo above Kokoro on blind preference, which means a listener prefers it about 4 times in 5, but the gap is in expressiveness and instruction-following, not intelligibility, where open models already match human read speech. GPUs buy training runs and concurrent streams, never a better individual sentence. Supporting many voices costs kilobytes when a voice is an embedding vector (Kokoro: 0.5 MB per voice, 54 voices in 27 MB) and switches per utterance for free; the platform voices feel limited because each is a self-contained 80–220 MB model that the app can neither download nor style. A prompt such as "talk like a 16-year-old Japanese boy speaking English" is accepted today by Qwen3-TTS-VoiceDesign, MOSS-VoiceGenerator and half a dozen hosted vendors, with the best open model following roughly five of every six style instructions, but no public benchmark measures perceived age or non-native accent fidelity, no permissive dataset contains Japanese-accented English or pre-teen speech, and ElevenLabs bans child-like voices outright. Building such a system is a credible three-month learning project (a description-to-speaker-embedding mapper on a frozen cloner, trained on CC0 Common Voice metadata) but not a worthwhile way to solve Math City's voice problem, which an afternoon with an existing voice-design model already solves.

## The built-in voices feel limited because each is a locked 100 MB model you cannot download or style

iOS exposes exactly three voice tiers through `AVSpeechSynthesisVoiceQuality`: `default`, which ships on the device, and `enhanced` and `premium`, which "you must download to use" ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfaudio/avspeechsynthesisvoicequality)). The download is manual, by the user, in Settings; `speechVoices()` lists only what is installed and Apple has given developers no API to enumerate or trigger the missing ones ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/758460)). Enhanced voices "can be 100 MB or larger" and need Wi-Fi ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-us/111798)); user reports put individual enhanced Siri voices at **148–217 MB** ([AppleVis](https://www.applevis.com/forum/ios-ipados/enhanced-quality-siri-voice)). Siri's own voices are off limits to third-party apps for anti-impersonation reasons ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/682438)), and Personal Voice is restricted to AAC apps ([WWDC23](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10033/)). There is no style or emotion parameter anywhere in `AVSpeechUtterance`; the public voice traits are only `isNoveltyVoice` and `isPersonalVoice` ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfaudio/avspeechsynthesisvoice)). One 2026-specific hazard: **iOS 26.0 and 26.1 return the system default instead of the user's chosen voice when a voice is requested by language**, so a voice must be addressed by explicit `identifier` ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/804648)). Math City's `TtsService` currently calls only `setLanguage('en-US')`, which is exactly the code path that regression hits.

Android is the same shape with more fragmentation. The `Voice` API gives a quality integer up to `QUALITY_VERY_HIGH` (500) and an `isNetworkConnectionRequired()` flag ([Android](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/speech/tts/Voice)), Google's high-quality offline packs were "200+MB" against 5–6 MB for the compact voice in the only measured data found ([Android Police](https://www.androidpolice.com/2014/03/05/google-releases-huge-text-to-speech-tts-for-android-update-v3-0-with-high-quality-voices-new-languages-and-ui-changes-apk-download/)), and the install is again a Settings trip the app can only point at. Worse for a kids' app on hand-me-down hardware: **Samsung TTS became inaccessible to third-party apps on Android 15 / One UI 7–8** ([Speech Central](https://speechcentral.net/2026/03/22/samsung-tts-missing-on-android-15-one-ui-7-8-whats-really-happening/)), and Fire tablets ship Amazon's own IVONA-derived engine with no Google TTS ([XDA](https://xdaforums.com/t/how-to-switch-fire-tablet-tts-engine-from-fire-internal-to-google-text-to-speech.3950193/)). Neither platform offers a child voice or an age knob; genuine children's TTS voices exist only commercially, from Acapela via AssistiveWare ([Acapela](https://www.acapela-group.com/voices/children-voices/)).

The reason these voices are so large and so inflexible is architectural: a platform voice is a self-contained per-voice acoustic model, the "one model per voice" pattern, which is also why a single Piper medium voice weighs 63–79 MB ([piper-voices](https://huggingface.co/rhasspy/piper-voices/blob/main/en/en_US/lessac/medium/en_US-lessac-medium.onnx)). `flutter_tts` 4.2.5 (MIT) faithfully exposes what the platforms allow (`getVoices`/`setVoice`, rate, pitch, volume, word-progress callbacks, `synthesizeToFile`) and hides what they do not (SSML, voice installation, any style control) ([pub.dev](https://pub.dev/packages/flutter_tts)). Both platforms do let an app register itself as a system TTS engine (Apple's `AVSpeechSynthesisProviderAudioUnit` extension since iOS 16/17, Android's `TextToSpeechService`), and sherpa-onnx ships a working Android example ([TtsEngine.kt](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx/blob/master/android/SherpaOnnxTtsEngine/app/src/main/java/com/k2fsa/sherpa/onnx/tts/engine/TtsEngine.kt)), but that path only matters if other apps should see the voice; for Math City's own use, running a model in-process is strictly simpler. The practical ceiling of the current approach is therefore: whatever default voices the device has, plus any enhanced voice a parent has installed, differentiated across ten citizens only by picking distinct installed voices and offsetting pitch and rate.

Three conclusions follow. First, only Kokoro and Pocket TTS combine permissive weights with an active ecosystem and documented mobile ports, and Kokoro is the only one with arena-grade quality evidence, ranking roughly 6th among open-weight models at ~1,060 Elo ([offlinetts](https://www.offlinetts.com/blog/tts-arena-leaderboard-2026/)). Second, the GPL risk lives entirely in the text front end, not the acoustic model; projects have filed real licence conflicts over kokoro-onnx pulling in GPLv3 `phonemizer` ([askwell #619](https://github.com/Rumeasiyan/askwell/issues/619)). Because Math City's text domain is closed (numbers, operators, a few hundred strings, and `spoken_text.dart` already rewrites symbols into words), restricting Kokoro to its Apache-licensed misaki dictionary ([misaki on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/misaki/)) or shipping a phoneme table precomputed offline would avoid bundling espeak-ng, though the maintainer's position on this was not confirmable ([hexgrad/kokoro #247](https://github.com/hexgrad/kokoro/issues/247)). Third, the store maths decides packaging: both stores allow 4 GB apps but treat ~200 MB as the practical line, for cellular download on iOS ([iDownloadBlog](https://www.idownloadblog.com/2019/05/31/app-store-cellular-download-limit-200-mb/)) and for the base module and "large app" warning on Play ([Android](https://developer.android.com/guide/app-bundle)). Kokoro int8 at ~107 MB can be bundled; Kokoro fp32 or Pocket TTS must go in a Play Asset Delivery pack or, on iOS, Background Assets, since **On-Demand Resources is deprecated as of iOS 27** ([App Store Connect](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/reference/app-uploads/on-demand-resources-size-limits)); a Flutter plugin covers both mechanisms ([asset_delivery](https://pub.dev/documentation/asset_delivery/latest/)).

The honest performance picture for Math City's hardware is thin. No source measured any neural TTS on any iPad, and Kokoro's own numbers say RTF 0.3–0.6 on 2023–24 flagships and RTF 2–3 on budget six-core Android ([VoxSherpa](https://github.com/CodeBySonu95/VoxSherpa-TTS)). On a hand-me-down A10/A12-class iPad, Kokoro on CPU is probably slower than real time and Piper or Kitten comfortably faster; that is an extrapolation, not a measurement, and it is the single most important thing to test before committing. The mitigation is structural: because prompts are generated from seeds, the next question's audio can be synthesised while the current one is on screen, which hides any RTF above 1 for everything except the first prompt. Finally, anything that runs `generate()` synchronously on the main isolate freezes Flame's game loop for the full synthesis time; inference belongs in an isolate with PCM streamed to a player such as `flutter_soloud` ([pub.dev](https://pub.dev/packages/flutter_soloud)), which would also mean two audio engines negotiating the iOS audio session alongside `flame_audio`.

## Hosted and GPU-class models buy expressiveness and steerability, not intelligibility; extra GPUs buy concurrency, not better sentences

The published scaling evidence says TTS does not follow an LLM-style power law. Amazon's BASE TTS found that "the scaling from 1K to 10K hours and 150M to 400M parameters appears to be the critical threshold for emergent abilities", after which gains diminish ([BASE TTS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.08093v2)); the Emilia paper put the data plateau around 100k hours per language for 0.5–1B models ([Emilia](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.15907)). Kokoro, 82M parameters trained on under 100 hours for ~500 A100-hours (about $400), topped the Hugging Face TTS Arena in January 2025 ahead of XTTS v2 (467M), MetaVoice (1.2B) and Fish Speech ([Medium](https://medium.com/data-science-in-your-pocket/kokoro-82m-the-best-tts-model-in-just-82-million-parameters-512b4ba4f94c); [hexgrad/Kokoro-82M](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M)), and on the 2026 Artificial Analysis board Microsoft's 7B VibeVoice (Elo 969) sits *below* Kokoro (~1,060) ([AA leaderboard](https://artificialanalysis.ai/text-to-speech/leaderboard)). Naturalness of short read speech saturates at tiny scale. What the frontier buys is different: on EmergentTTS-Eval, a judge-scored benchmark of expressive and hard text, Gemini 2.5 Flash TTS wins 75.6% of comparisons against gpt-4o-mini-tts, the best open model (Higgs Audio v2) 38.9%, and Kokoro 25.9% ([EmergentTTS-Eval](https://github.com/boson-ai/EmergentTTS-Eval-public)); the benchmark authors summarise open models as showing "flat intonation, error-prone code-switching, and limited expressive range" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23009)). For Math City that maps cleanly: a Kokoro-class model reads "What is seven times eight?" at arena-competitive quality; where scale would show is a letter from Mr. Grumbold that needs to sound put-upon.

On blind preference the frontier is Eleven v4 at **1,319–1,321 Elo** (launched 28 Sep 2026), Qwen-Audio-3.1-TTS-Plus 1,292, Cartesia Sonic 3.6 1,276–1,278 and Gemini 3.8 Flash TTS 1,267–1,275 ([progressiverobot](https://www.progressiverobot.com/2026/09/28/eleven-v4-turbo-elevenlabs-tops-tts-arena/); [cellcog](https://cellcog.ai/blog/eleven-v4/)). Standard Elo arithmetic turns a 260-point gap over Kokoro into an **82% head-to-head preference**, and Sonic 3.6's ~216-point gap into 78%. Independently measured first-audio latency for the fast hosted tiers is 185–208 ms (ElevenLabs Flash), ~270 ms (Sonic 3.5) and 290–323 ms (Deepgram Aura-2), with vendor datasheets consistently 2–3x lower than those p50s ([gradium](https://gradium.ai/content/tts-latency-benchmark-2026); [futureagi](https://futureagi.com/blog/best-tts-providers-voice-agents-2026/)), and a mobile network round trip sits on top, so an on-device model at 100–300 ms usually feels faster.

Cost is where hosting dies for a free app. List prices span three orders of magnitude, from hosted Kokoro at $0.62 per million characters ([DeepInfra](https://deepinfra.com/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M/api)) through the $16/M cloud "neural" tiers and Eleven v4 Turbo at $40/M to Eleven v4 at $80/M and Google Studio at $160/M ([Puter](https://developer.puter.com/tutorials/elevenlabs-api-pricing/); [diyai](https://diyai.io/ai-tools/audio-generation/google-cloud-text-to-speech-pricing/)). At an assumed 300 prompts of ~60 characters per child per week for 10,000 children, that is 180M characters a week: **$112/week on Kokoro, ~$2,900 on the $16 tiers, $7,200 on Eleven v4 Turbo, $14,400 on Eleven v4**, or roughly $0.75–0.94M a year at ElevenLabs prices. Pre-rendering inverts the arithmetic: 50,000 distinct strings of 60 characters is 3M characters, a one-time $240 even at $80/M, or free inside Google's 1M-characters-per-month Chirp 3 HD tier spread over three months. The hosted-versus-on-device question for Math City is therefore really "pre-render with a strong voice and bundle the audio" versus "ship a model and synthesise live", and only live-generated text forces the second.

Multiple GPUs enter TTS in exactly two places, training and throughput. F5-TTS trained for over a week on 8xA100 80G ([F5-TTS](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.06885v1)); Fish Speech spent a week on 8xH100 plus a week on 8xRTX 4090 for the vocoder ([Fish-Speech](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.01156v1)); Kokoro needed ~1,000 A100-hours total. In serving, Baseten runs Orpheus-3B at 16–24 simultaneous real-time streams per H100 MIG slice after TensorRT-LLM optimisation ([Baseten](https://www.baseten.co/blog/canopy-labs-selects-baseten-as-preferred-inference-provider-for-orpheus-tts-model/)), and vLLM-Omni serves Qwen3-TTS at ~43 audio-seconds per second across two H20s at 64 concurrent requests ([alphasignal](https://alphasignal.ai/news/vllm-omni-squeezes-172-more-audio-out-of-four-speech-models)). No open TTS model is large enough to need tensor parallelism for a single utterance: the largest (Higgs Audio v2 at 5.8B, VibeVoice 7B) fit one 24 GB card in bf16, and autoregressive TTS is latency-bound by sequential decoding plus a codec stage where inter-GPU sync adds delay. A field report makes the point vividly: Qwen3-TTS streaming plateaued at ~12 concurrent sessions with the accelerator at 26% utilisation ("every resource we can measure is idle"), and splitting pipeline stages across two chips gained ~9% ([vllm-omni #7993](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm-omni/issues/7993)). For Math City, one GPU versus many is a question about how fast a build machine can pre-render a curriculum, and a single 4090-class card serving a dozen streams renders tens of thousands of short prompts an hour.

## Open versus proprietary: ~100 Elo at the top, ~375 Elo for what Math City may legally ship

The headline gap is small and shrinking. On the Artificial Analysis arena the best open-weight model trailed commercial leaders by 223 Elo in 2023 and 81 Elo by mid-2026 ([pinggy](https://pinggy.io/blog/best_open_source_self_hosted_text_to_speech_models/)); Breeze TTS 2 reached 1,215–1,216 on 25 Aug 2026 and passed ElevenLabs' then-flagship Eleven v3 (1,177), the first open model to do so, before Eleven v4 restored a ~100-point lead a month later. Fish Audio S2 Pro sits at ~1,117–1,125 and Kokoro at ~1,056–1,062. On objective intelligibility the gap closed earlier: against a human reference of 2.14% English WER on Seed-TTS-eval, Qwen3-TTS-0.6B scores 1.64 and VoxCPM 1.85, while ElevenLabs Multilingual v2 posts the best WER (1.43) but by far the lowest speaker similarity (50.0 versus 65–68 for open clones) ([CosyVoice README](https://github.com/FunAudioLLM/CosyVoice); [Higgs README_V2](https://github.com/boson-ai/higgs-audio/blob/main/README_V2.md)). On MiniMax's multilingual test ElevenLabs scores 2.339 English WER against Qwen3-TTS's 0.934 ([VoxCPM README](https://github.com/OpenBMB/VoxCPM)). Intelligibility parity arrived in 2024–25, speaker-similarity advantage for open clones in 2025, preference parity with ElevenLabs' previous flagship in August 2026; the remaining measurable gap is expressiveness, where the best open model trails Gemini by ~35 win-rate points.

The licensing picture is what matters for this project, and it is bimodal. Every open model at the top of the preference charts is non-commercial: Breeze TTS 2 under a BreezeBlue Research and Non-Commercial licence ([HF LICENSE](https://huggingface.co/BreezeBlue/Breeze-TTS-2/blob/main/LICENSE)), Fish S2 Pro under the Fish Audio Research License that explicitly counts "distributing Your product or service" as commercial advantage ([LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fishaudio/fish-speech/main/LICENSE)), Higgs Audio v3 under Boson's non-commercial licence ([README](https://github.com/boson-ai/higgs-audio)), Voxtral TTS CC-BY-NC ([MarkTechPost](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/03/28/mistral-ai-releases-voxtral-tts-a-4b-open-weight-streaming-speech-model-for-low-latency-multilingual-voice-generation/)), F5-TTS CC-BY-NC "due to the training data Emilia" ([F5-TTS](https://github.com/SWivid/F5-TTS)), Fish S1-mini CC-BY-NC-SA, XTTS-v2 under a non-commercial CPML with no commercial path since Coqui closed in December 2023. A free, no-ads game does not escape these: the Fish and Breeze definitions reach any distributed product. The models that are permissive for both code and weights, verified from repository LICENSE files, are **Qwen3-TTS 0.6B/1.7B (Apache-2.0), VoxCPM/VoxCPM2 (Apache-2.0), Chatterbox 500M/Turbo 350M/Nano 110M (MIT), MOSS-TTS 1.7B–8B and MOSS-TTS-Nano 100M (Apache-2.0), CosyVoice 2/3 (Apache-2.0 repo), Kyutai TTS and Pocket TTS (CC-BY-4.0 weights), Orpheus 3B (Apache-2.0 with inherited Llama terms), Dia/Dia2, Sesame CSM, Zonos, Parler-TTS, Kani, Marvis and Tencent AuK** ([Qwen3-TTS LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS/main/LICENSE); [VoxCPM](https://github.com/OpenBMB/VoxCPM); [Chatterbox LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/resemble-ai/chatterbox/master/LICENSE); [MOSS-TTS](https://github.com/OpenMOSS/MOSS-TTS); [Parler-TTS](https://github.com/huggingface/parler-tts)). A third group is commercial-OK but on bespoke terms outside the project's MIT/Apache/BSD/CC-BY/CC0 list: IndexTTS-2.5 under a bilibili licence that forbids using it to improve other models ([LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/index-tts/index-tts/main/LICENSE)), NVIDIA Magpie under the NVIDIA Open Model License, Supertonic under OpenRAIL-M, KittenTTS 2 under the Stellon Labs Community License, NeuTTS Nano under "NeuTTS Open License 1.0".

The best permissively licensed model with a published arena score is Qwen3-TTS at ~930–944 Elo ([AA Qwen3 TTS](https://artificialanalysis.ai/text-to-speech/models/qwen3-tts-vc-realtime)), roughly **375 Elo below Eleven v4**, which is a ~90% pairwise preference for the frontier. Two cautions soften that number. Arena votes are cast on general adult prompts and a listener cohort that is not children; and vendor-run blind tests point the other way, with a Resemble-commissioned Podonos study of 2,500 ratings finding 65.3% preferred Chatterbox Turbo over ElevenLabs Turbo v2.5 ([resemble.ai](https://www.resemble.ai/learn/models/chatterbox-turbo)), though no independent 2026 blind test between a permissive open model and a frontier vendor exists. The defensible reading is that pre-rendering with a 2026 Apache model produces speech at or above ElevenLabs on intelligibility and speaker-similarity metrics and roughly at ElevenLabs' 2024–25 tier on listener preference, with the current frontier API-only. Parler-TTS deserves one footnote as the only model whose *training data* provenance is fully permissive (LibriTTS-R and MLS), at the cost of 2024-era quality.

Robustness on Math City's actual text is the one dimension nobody benchmarks. LLM-codec TTS is documented to hallucinate on extremely short inputs ([arXiv 2406.17957](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.17957)); Dia's authors warn that inputs under about five seconds of audio "will sound unnatural" ([Dia](https://github.com/nari-labs/dia)), Marvis hallucinates "specially for new words or short sentences" ([Marvis](https://github.com/Marvis-Labs/marvis-tts)), and Orpheus and OuteTTS need repetition penalties for stability. On Seed-TTS "test-hard", the standard stability subset, Fun-CosyVoice3-RL leads open models at 5.44 CER ([CosyVoice README](https://github.com/FunAudioLLM/CosyVoice)); on EmergentTTS-Eval's "Questions" category Higgs Audio v2 beats gpt-4o-mini-tts 55.7% of the time while most open models lose ([Higgs README_V2](https://github.com/boson-ai/higgs-audio/blob/main/README_V2.md)). For a batch pre-render this is manageable: write numbers out in words (which `spokenFormOf` already does), generate two or three candidates per line and auto-select by ASR word error rate, and prefer models with phoneme or IPA overrides (MOSS-TTS, IndexTTS-2.5) for symbols. For live on-device synthesis it is another argument for the non-autoregressive Kokoro/Piper class, which has no repetition loops to fall into.

## Many voices cost kilobytes when the voice is a vector, and switch per utterance for free

The marginal cost of one more voice depends entirely on where the voice lives. In a single-speaker model it is a whole model: Piper's `en_US-lessac-medium.onnx` is 63.2 MB and `libritts_r-medium` 78.6 MB, so ten citizens and a narrator as separate Piper voices would be ~700 MB ([piper-voices](https://huggingface.co/rhasspy/piper-voices/blob/main/en/en_US/lessac/medium/en_US-lessac-medium.onnx)). In a speaker-ID table it is one row: VITS uses a 256-float `gin_channels` embedding ([vits config](https://github.com/jaywalnut310/vits/blob/main/configs/vctk_base.json)), so the 904-speaker `libritts_r-medium` carries all its speakers in under a megabyte and is only ~15 MB larger than the single-speaker file ([sherpa docs](https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa/blob/master/docs/source/onnx/tts/pretrained_models/vits.rst)). In a reference-style model it is a small tensor: a Kokoro voicepack is a `[510, 1, 256]` float32 array, **522,240 bytes per voice**, of which a single 256-float row (1 KB) is used per utterance, and all 54 voices fit in 27 MB ([cstr/kokoro-voices-GGUF](https://huggingface.co/cstr/kokoro-voices-GGUF); [kokoro model.py](https://github.com/hexgrad/kokoro/blob/main/kokoro/model.py)). In a zero-shot model it is a few seconds of audio plus a cached latent, but the model itself is 0.1–1.7B parameters. In a LoRA it is a 5–50 MB adapter that is useless without the base.

| Mechanism | +1 voice on disk | Switch per utterance? | Developer can mint a new voice offline? | Permissive example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| One model per voice (Piper single, platform voices) | 63–79 MB (Piper); 80–220 MB (Apple) | needs a model load | yes, by training (GPL pipeline, GPU hours) | Piper voices from CC-BY data |
| Speaker-ID table (Piper/VITS multi) | ~1 KB row | yes, integer `sid` | only by retraining the whole model | libritts_r / vctk |
| Reference/style vector (Kokoro, StyleTTS2) | 0.52 MB pack | yes, pass a tensor | by blending existing packs | Kokoro, Apache-2.0 |
| Zero-shot prompt (Chatterbox, Qwen3-TTS, Pocket TTS) | a 3–10 s WAV + cached latent | yes if latent cached | yes, from a recording | MIT / Apache / CC-BY |
| LoRA per voice | 5–50 MB adapter | adapter swap | yes, GPU fine-tune | depends on base |

Every mechanism except one-model-per-voice switches voices with no reload, because the acoustic weights are shared and the voice is just another input tensor; XTTS's documentation says the number of reference clips has no effect on runtime once latents are cached ([coqui xtts.md](https://github.com/coqui-ai/TTS/blob/dev/docs/source/models/xtts.md)). So the answer to "does supporting many voices make the model much bigger" is no for any modern architecture, and yes only for the Piper/platform pattern Math City is currently stuck with.

Blending is a first-class operation wherever a voice is a vector. The Kokoro voice that topped the TTS Arena was itself a 50/50 mix of two base voices, computed as a plain mean of tensors ([unfoldai](https://unfoldai.com/kokoro-82m/)); Tortoise averages conditioning latents to produce "what it thinks the 'average' of those two voices sounds like" ([tortoise](https://github.com/neonbjb/tortoise-tts/blob/main/Advanced_Usage.md)); model-merging research finds same-gender interpolations keep naturalness comparable to the base voices ([arXiv 2407.00766](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.00766)), with spherical interpolation the safer choice for normalised speaker-verification embeddings ([VoxMorph](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.20883)). Ten distinct citizens can therefore be minted from Kokoro's 54 voices at zero model cost, each a half-megabyte file.

What blending cannot do is make a child. No open, permissively licensed model or voice pack with a genuine child voice was found: Kokoro's catalogue, Piper's English list, Kitten's eight and Pocket TTS's 24 are all adult ([Piper VOICES.md](https://github.com/rhasspy/piper/blob/master/VOICES.md); [pocket-tts](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/pocket-tts)). Pitch shifting is the standard commercial fake ([speechgen.io](https://speechgen.io/en/child-tts/)) and a known data-augmentation trick that makes speech sound "as if ... spoken by a different speaker" ([arXiv 2406.10284](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.10284)), but no controlled naturalness study of pitch-shifted adult TTS was located, and a time-domain pitch shift leaves adult formants in place, the classic chipmunk artefact. Teen voices are more tractable than young children: Mozilla Common Voice is CC0 and its English v27 release has **2,826.8 validated hours from 100,546 speakers with 5.89% self-labelled "teens"**, roughly 165 hours ([cv-corpus-27.0](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/common-voice/cv-dataset/main/datasets/scripted-speech/cv-corpus-27.0-2026-09-11.json)), while every English children's corpus is research-restricted: MyST is CC-BY-NC-SA with a separate commercial licence ([MyST](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.13347)), CMU Kids is under LDC agreements ([LDC97S63](https://catalog.ldc.upenn.edu/LDC97S63)), CHILDES is CC-BY-NC-SA ([arXiv 2607.03670](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.03670)). For Maya, "age 7, expert climber", the realistic options are a light, higher-pitched adult register from a designed voice, or a consenting adult actor doing a character voice under a written release.

## Baked, cloned, or prompted: "a 16-year-old Japanese boy speaking English" is promptable but unmeasured

Three ways exist to get a voice. Baked-in presets are what the platforms, Kokoro, Piper and Kitten offer. Cloning from a sample is now near-universal in the LLM-codec class and works from 3–10 seconds of audio, with speaker similarity at or above the human same-speaker baseline (CosyVoice 3 at 0.78 against a human cross-recording baseline of 0.755; Qwen3-TTS 0.79–0.83) ([CosyVoice 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.17589); [Qwen3-TTS](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS)). That quality is exactly why cloning now carries legal weight: Tennessee's ELVIS Act (effective 1 July 2024) makes unauthorised commercial voice replicas actionable ([ELVIS Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ELVIS_Act)), EU AI Act Article 50 obligations went live in August 2026 and require machine-readable marking of synthetic audio ([transparentaudio](https://www.transparentaudio.ai/resources/the-2025-transparency-rulebook-for-voice-ai)), ElevenLabs prohibits uploading "Voice Data from children under the age of 18" ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/privacy-policy)), and Kyutai's and Neuphonic's terms forbid cloning without consent. For a cast of fictional citizens, cloning real people buys nothing and risks everything; design is the right tool.

Designing a voice from text is, as of October 2026, a commoditised product feature. ElevenLabs Voice Design v3 takes "age, accent, tone, pacing, and audio quality in a single sentence" ([ElevenLabs docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-creative/voices/voice-design)); Hume, MiniMax, Inworld, Fish Audio and Gradium (spun out of Kyutai, launched September 2026, claiming 83.4% prompt adherence on InstructTTSEval and "over 100 accents") all return a persistent `voice_id` from a description ([MarkTechPost](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/09/09/gradium-launches-voice-design-write-a-prompt-get-a-brand-new-synthetic-voice-in-seconds/)). A second camp (OpenAI gpt-4o-mini-tts, Gemini TTS, Azure Dragon HD, Cartesia) only steers the *delivery* of preset voices and cannot create a new identity; OpenAI's model "doesn't officially support explicit accent selection" and users report it cannot reliably produce even a British accent ([OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/british-accent-and-pronuciation-in-gpt-4o-mini-tts/1150812)). Among open weights, **Qwen3-TTS-12Hz-1.7B-VoiceDesign (Apache-2.0)** accepts "timbre, persona, age, emotion, pace, pitch, and delivery" in free text across ten languages including Japanese, and its own README example describes a speaker as "Male, 17 years old, tenor range, gaining confidence" ([Qwen3-TTS README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS/main/README.md)); MOSS-VoiceGenerator (1.7B, Apache-2.0) and VoiceSculptor (Apache-2.0) do the same, and Parler-TTS (Apache-2.0) did it in 2024 with coarser attributes ([MOSS-TTS](https://github.com/OpenMOSS/MOSS-TTS); [VoiceSculptor](https://github.com/ASLP-lab/VoiceSculptor)).

How well these follow a compound prompt is measured, at coarse granularity, by InstructTTSEval. On the English set Qwen3-TTS-VoiceDesign scores 82.9/82.4/68.4 across its three tasks against Gemini Flash's 92.3/93.8/80.1 ([Qwen3-TTS README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS/main/README.md)), so the best open model misses roughly one instruction in six and the frontier roughly one in twelve. But the benchmark scores acoustic parameters and style descriptions, not demographic accuracy: **no public evaluation measures whether a designed voice sounds 16 rather than 25, or whether "Japanese-accented English" comes out as a recognisable L2 accent rather than a caricature.** The data explains why. Every captioning pipeline that produces training descriptions (Data-Speech, ParaSpeechCaps, CapSpeech) labels age no finer than decade buckets, and Data-Speech lists accent classification as a TODO ([dataspeech](https://github.com/huggingface/dataspeech)). L2-ARCTIC, the standard non-native English corpus, covers Arabic, Hindi, Korean, Mandarin, Spanish and Vietnamese speakers, **not Japanese**, and is CC-BY-NC ([L2-ARCTIC](https://psi.engr.tamu.edu/l2-arctic-corpus-docs/)); UME-ERJ, 202 Japanese speakers reading English, is research-only ([NII SRC](https://research.nii.ac.jp/src/en/UME-ERJ.html)); the Speech Accent Archive is CC-BY-NC-SA. The only permissive source of Japanese-accented English is Common Voice's free-text accent field, whose Japanese count is unknown. Expect "teens / young adult" resolution at best, and expect a model with sparse L2 data to snap "Japanese accent" onto a stereotype; FAccT 2025 work on Speechify and ElevenLabs found performance disparities across English accents that "may inadvertently reinforce linguistic privilege" ([arXiv 2504.09346](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09346)), and a 2026 ACM paper found a leading commercial TTS "disproportionately reproduces white, male, US-accented speech when prompted to convey competence" ([ACM](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772363.3798657)).

One policy fact bears directly on Math City's cast: **ElevenLabs bars "children's voices or voices that sound child-like" from its library, explicitly including "adult voices designed to mimic or sound like children"** ([ElevenLabs help center](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/help-center/product/voices/voice-library/can-childrens-or-child-like-voices-be-added-to-the-voice-library)). A seven-year-old citizen cannot be designed there. Identity consistency across utterances, finally, is an engineering property rather than a modelling one: every vendor freezes a `voice_id`, VoiceSculptor separates design from a cloning stage that re-uses the designed waveform ([alphaxiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2601.10629)), and reviewers still report designed voices "drift slightly across very long sessions" on ElevenLabs ([eesel](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/elevenlabs-reviews)). Any pipeline Math City adopts should design once, then pin the result as a reference clip or embedding.

## Recommendation for Math City

**Do now, with no new dependencies.** Make the ten citizens sound different on the engines you already use. Enumerate `getVoices`, prefer any installed `enhanced`/`premium` (iOS) or high-`quality` offline (Android) English voice, assign each citizen a distinct installed voice **by identifier, never by language** (the iOS 26 regression), and layer per-citizen pitch and rate offsets: Maya higher and quicker, Mr. Grumbold lower and slower, the narrator at today's 0.45. Pre-warm with a silent utterance at launch so the first Google HQ voice load does not stall. Add a one-time, parent-facing "install a better voice" tip that deep-links to Settings, since the app cannot install one. This is a modest change to `TtsService` and `citizen.dart` and it is the only lever platform TTS offers; it will not make Maya sound seven, and nothing short of a custom model will.

**Pre-render the letters; synthesise the questions live.** The two text streams have opposite shapes. Letters are a finite, hand-written corpus (chapter one has a handful) that never changes at runtime; question prompts are generated from seeds in effectively unbounded surface forms and must stay live. Render every letter offline, on a build machine, with **Qwen3-TTS-1.7B-VoiceDesign** (Apache-2.0, ~4.5 GB bf16, runs on an 8 GB GPU or a 16 GB Mac through mlx-audio ([mlx-audio](https://github.com/Blaizzy/mlx-audio))): write one persona description per citizen, generate a few candidates, pick by ear and by ASR word error rate, then pin each chosen voice as a reference clip so later letters stay consistent. VoxCPM2 (Apache-2.0, voice design plus controllable cloning) is the alternative if Qwen's output disappoints; Chatterbox Turbo (MIT) adds `[chuckle]`-style tags if warmth matters more than persona range. Ship the audio as compressed files (tens of letters times a few seconds is single-digit megabytes) and play them through the `flame_audio` engine already in the app. This yields voices ~375 Elo short of Eleven v4 but at or above ElevenLabs on intelligibility, for zero dollars and zero runtime risk. One cost to decide consciously: pre-rendered audio loses the word-by-word highlighting that `TtsService.progress` drives from the engine's range callbacks. Accept sentence-level highlighting for letters (the text is prose, not a sum), or keep live TTS as the fallback path whenever a bundled clip is missing, which also covers future letters before they are rendered.

**Plan, but do not yet build, an on-device neural pack for questions.** If the platform voices remain the weak point after the changes above, the candidate is **Kokoro-82M int8 via `sherpa_onnx`** (Apache runtime, Apache weights): ~80 MB model plus 27 MB for all 54 voices, which fits under both stores' 200 MB lines and can be bundled rather than downloaded. Avoid the GPL front end by constraining Math City's closed vocabulary to misaki's dictionary or shipping a precomputed phoneme table, and confirm the maintainer's position first. Design the ten citizen voices by blending Kokoro packs (half a megabyte each). Run inference in an isolate, stream PCM, and pre-synthesise the next seeded question while the current one is on screen. The gate before any of this is a measurement the research could not supply: **Kokoro's real-time factor and memory on the oldest iPad Math City supports**, since RTF above 1 on budget hardware is documented on Android and plausible on A10/A12-class iPads. Pocket TTS (CC-BY-4.0, no phonemizer, cloning, six releases this year) is the fallback if Kokoro's licence or speed fails, at the price of a ~242 MB asset pack. Shipping larger packs requires Play Asset Delivery and, on iOS, Background Assets rather than the deprecated On-Demand Resources; both are store-hosted, which keeps "no backend" intact in spirit but is a judgement call the developer should make explicitly.

**Avoid, and why.** Live hosted TTS of any kind: the cost is $150k–$1M a year at 10,000 weekly players, it requires a network and a backend, and it moves children's requests through a vendor, triggering COPPA diligence everywhere and outright age bans at ElevenLabs (no under-18s, no "bundled solutions that target anyone under the age of 13" ([ElevenLabs use policy](https://elevenlabs.io/use-policy))) and Hume (18+ ([Hume terms](https://www.hume.ai/terms-of-use))). ElevenLabs output even when pre-rendered, because the "bundled solutions" clause reads as covering shipped audio and the child-like voice ban blocks Maya; Google Chirp 3 HD within its free tier is the one hosted pre-render route whose terms appear workable, if the developer wants a ~200-Elo uplift over Kokoro for letters and is comfortable with a build-time API dependency. Every non-commercial or research-only model regardless of how good it sounds: F5-TTS, Fish S1-mini and S2 Pro, Breeze TTS 2, Higgs Audio v3, Voxtral TTS, Llasa, MaskGCT, OuteTTS 1.0, XTTS-v2, and VibeVoice's "research and development purposes only" advisory. Every bespoke licence outside the project's list: IndexTTS (bilibili), Supertonic (OpenRAIL-M, and archived anyway), KittenTTS 2 (Stellon), NeuTTS Nano, Magpie (NVIDIA OML). Piper, unless the project decides GPL-3 in the bundle is acceptable *and* reads past VOICES.md's "intended for personal use and text to speech research only" ([VOICES.md](https://github.com/OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl/blob/main/docs/VOICES.md)). Cloning any real person, and any children's speech corpus, for both licence and consent reasons; citizen voices should be designed from text or recorded by consenting adults under a written release that covers synthetic reproduction. Whatever is chosen, add a credits line stating that the voices are computer-generated, which is where Azure's guidance for products aimed at minors and the EU Article 50 logic both land ([Azure transparency note](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/responsible-ai/speech-service/text-to-speech/transparency-note)).

## The ML side project, frankly

As a way to give Math City its voices, building a prompt-to-voice system is not worthwhile. The problem is solved for adult voices with common native accents: Qwen3-TTS-VoiceDesign, MOSS-VoiceGenerator, VoiceSculptor and Gradium already exist, Parler-TTS Mini alone cost roughly 1,150 H100-hours to train (4 nodes of 8 H100s for about 1.5 days ([parler-tts training](https://github.com/huggingface/parler-tts/blob/main/training/README.md))), and Math City needs ten fixed voices, which is a one-off design session with an existing model, not a system. Nothing one person builds in a year will beat Qwen's 1.7B on general quality, and the project's shipping constraint (CC0/CC-BY/Apache data only) excludes every richly captioned corpus that makes such models good: ParaSpeechCaps, CapSpeech, EARS and Expresso are all non-commercial.

As a learning project with a genuine research niche, it is well-posed and tractable on one consumer GPU, provided the scope is the part of the problem that is still open: fine-grained age, non-native accents, and one-description-to-many-distinct-voices diversity, plus the complete absence of an evaluation for any of them. The credible three-month shape is a **description-to-speaker-embedding mapper on top of a frozen zero-shot cloner**: take Zonos (Apache-2.0, 6 GB VRAM, conditions on a speaker embedding ([Zonos](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Zyphra/Zonos/main/README.md))) or Chatterbox Turbo (MIT, 350M), extract embeddings for the ~100k Common Voice English speakers that carry age, gender and self-reported accent (CC0), template captions from the metadata in the Data-Speech style, and train a small text encoder with a flow-matching head so one prompt yields a distribution of voices rather than one. This is the pattern of Chen et al. (Interspeech 2024), DreamVoice and the 2026 ProPS work ([ISCA](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2024/chen24q_interspeech.html); [DreamVoice](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.16314); [ProPS](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05276)), the mapper is tens of millions of parameters, and embedding extraction plus training fits in a GPU-day or two. Paired with a small perceptual test set (prompts rated for perceived age and accent by L1-Japanese and child-development-aware raters, plus automatic age and accent classifiers), it would be a real contribution, because no such benchmark exists. The twelve-month extension is an age- and accent-aware LoRA fine-tune of Parler-Mini or, once a recipe with description fields appears, Qwen3-TTS-VoiceDesign, on Common Voice plus GLOBE plus Emilia-YODAS (CC-BY-4.0), evaluated on InstructTTSEval-EN and the custom set.

Set expectations accordingly. "16-year-old" will resolve to "teen" at best, because the data does. "Japanese boy speaking English" depends on how many Common Voice contributors self-labelled a Japanese accent, a number nobody has counted and which is plausibly a few hundred. Children under about 12 are out of reach with permissive data and are the one voice type the big vendors refuse to make. The honest deliverable to Math City from the whole exercise would be ten fixed embeddings or reference clips, which is also what an afternoon with Qwen3-TTS-VoiceDesign delivers. Do it to learn speech ML and to publish an evaluation the field lacks; do not do it on the critical path of the game.

## Assumptions, and the open questions that would change the answer

This report assumes English only; fully offline play with no network dependency at runtime; no backend operated by the project, with store-hosted asset packs treated as a grey area to be decided; an audience of 6–14-year-olds, so child-safety terms and COPPA apply even though TTS of app-authored text carries no child data by construction; permissive licences only, meaning MIT/Apache/BSD/CC-BY/CC0 with CC-BY-NC, research-only and bespoke community licences excluded and GPL treated as a separate decision rather than a prohibition; a free app with no revenue, so per-request hosted pricing is compared against zero; question prompts generated from seeds and letters hand-written and finite; and target hardware that includes hand-me-down iPads of unknown vintage. It also assumes, where the notes relied on search snippets of blocked pages (Hugging Face model cards, the Artificial Analysis table, vendor terms), that those snippets are accurate; the licences of CosyVoice 3, Spark-TTS and Higgs Audio v2 weights, the OpenRAIL-M restriction text for Supertonic, and ElevenLabs' exact "bundled solutions" wording should be read at source before anything ships.

Eight questions would move the recommendation. Whether GPL-3 code in the app bundle is acceptable: if yes, Piper and an espeak-ng front end for Kokoro open up, subject to Piper's "personal use" statement. The oldest iPad and its RAM that Math City must support: if Kokoro int8 is slower than real time there, the on-device path narrows to Piper/Kitten-class models or disappears in favour of pre-rendering. Whether letters, hints or wrong-answer explanations will ever be generated at runtime rather than authored: if so, pre-rendering stops being sufficient and an on-device model becomes mandatory. Whether Apple- or Google-hosted asset packs count as "a backend": this decides whether anything over ~100 MB is shippable at all. Whether sentence-level highlighting is acceptable for letters: if word-level highlighting is non-negotiable, pre-rendered audio needs word timestamps and the design gets harder. Whether a build-time call to a hosted API (Google Chirp 3 HD within the free tier) is acceptable for pre-rendering: it buys ~200 Elo over Kokoro for the letters at the cost of a vendor dependency in the build. Whether the Kokoro maintainer confirms that an espeak-free English pipeline is supported for a closed vocabulary. And whether Math City will ever leave English: Kokoro covers nine languages and Pocket TTS seven, while Piper's per-voice model and the platform engines fragment further with every locale.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the question Math City started with. "Which TTS model should the app use" turns out to be two questions with different answers, because the app speaks two kinds of text: an unbounded stream of templated prompts that only a live engine can read, and a small, fixed set of character letters that a build machine can render once with a model far better than anything that will run on a 2018 iPad. The economics and the policy both push the same way: the frontier is API-only, expensive, and in the case of ElevenLabs and Hume closed to children by its own terms, while the permissive open models of 2026 have reached intelligibility parity and sit a year or so behind on expressiveness, which is exactly the dimension that matters for a letter and barely matters for a sum.

The deeper shift is that voice identity has become cheap. A voice used to be a 100 MB model nobody but Apple or Google could make; it is now a half-megabyte vector that can be blended, a ten-second clip that can be cloned with legal consequences, or a sentence of English that can be turned into a speaker. The one thing that has not become cheap, and will not until permissively licensed data exists, is a convincing child or a faithful non-native accent. Math City should treat its seven-year-old citizen as a design constraint to be met with a light adult register and honest credits, not as a modelling problem to be solved, and should spend any ML curiosity on the evaluation the field is missing rather than on the voices the game already can have.
